Day 1: Studio 2 – The Acoustic Foundation and Brush Drums

Terence Law — Recording & Mixing Engineer Session Log

The Final Session: Building Emma Slany’s Vision from Scratch

By Terence Law

For the final artist of my Major Project, I worked with Emma Slany, a singer-songwriter and guitarist introduced to me by Joey. Unlike some artists who arrive with fully fleshed-out arrangements, Emma wanted to build her song completely from the ground up in the studio. This is a fantastic way to work; it removes the pressure of pre-production and relies heavily on the environment to spark those spontaneous "eureka" moments.

Here is a technical breakdown of how we constructed Emma’s dark, acoustic-driven track over two days, navigating everything from modified drum miking to unconventional guitar slides.

Emma sent me a simple voice memo demo the day before our session. The song had a distinctly dark, moody character, which heavily influenced my microphone selection for the foundational acoustic guitar tracks.

The Acoustic Guitar Array

To capture the track's inherently dark warmth while maintaining a wide stereo image, I utilized a multi-mic array blending ribbon and condenser microphones. As Mike Senior (2010) notes in his acoustic guitar masterclass, utilizing different microphone topologies allows an engineer to balance the warm, woody resonance of the instrument's body with the articulate transient details of the strings.

  • Stereo Image (Ribbons): Two Coles 4038s panned hard left and right. I placed the left mic near the neck where she voiced the chords. Crucially, following Senior's (2010) advice to avoid the intense, muddy low-end build-up directly in front of the soundhole, I positioned the right ribbon slightly below the soundhole to capture a balanced, warm body resonance.

  • Clarity (Condenser): Neumann U87. Because ribbon microphones naturally roll off high frequencies, I placed a large-diaphragm condenser near the 12th fret to inject pristine top-end clarity back into the center of the mix.

To build a massive acoustic wall, I requested a double track (DT). However, rather than using Emma's primary guitar, we switched to a Yamaha F310. Blending the unique resonant properties of two different acoustic guitars creates a lush, phase-rich chorus effect, mimicking the sound of a 12-string guitar (Coodley, 2013; Lewis, no date).

After capturing a quick scratch vocal using a Neumann U87, we brought Joey in to track drums and bass.

Drums: The Modified Glyn Johns Approach

Joey's drum part relied heavily on playing the snare with brushes. Brushes generate very low sound pressure levels (SPL). To capture this delicate articulation, I had to modify the traditional Glyn Johns technique (Gasson, n.d.; Hirst, 2015; Robjohns, 2003).

  • Kick In: Shure SM7B

A large-diaphragm dynamic mic placed off axis inside the kick to capture the soft, pillowy thud of the beater while offering excellent off-axis rejection from the rest of the kit.

  • Snare Top: Shure SM57

The tailored presence peak of the 57 is perfect for highlighting the high-frequency "swish" and scrape of the wire brushes against the batter head.

  • The Modified Glyn Johns Technique:

I utilized a Glyn Johns overhead configuration, but heavily modified the distances. As Hugh Robjohns (2003) and Gasson stress, the strict "3-foot" rule of Glyn Johns must serve the physics of the session. Because brushes produce very low sound pressure, leaving the mics high in the air would capture too much room noise and not enough direct source signal. I brought both overheads significantly closer to the kit to increase the direct-to-reverberant ratio. However, adhering strictly to Robjohns' (2003) acoustic principles, I ensured both overheads remained perfectly equidistant from the center of the snare to guarantee absolute phase coherence.

  • Room: Coles 4038. Placed right in front of the kit.

Engineering Philosophy: I moved the overheads closer to maximize the signal-to-noise ratio of the soft brush strokes. While I could have simply cranked the gain on our Neve 1073 preamps, doing so would have committed heavy analog texture and saturation to the recording. Because we were writing the arrangement on the fly, I needed to leave myself a clean, uncolored signal with plenty of "wiggle room" for the mix phase. Conversely, on the Coles 4038 room mic, I intentionally boosted the preamp gain heavily to capture an aggressive ambient layer.

Bongos and Control Room Overdubs

Next came an experimental bongo section, with both Emma and Joey playing them simultaneously to mimic the sound of raindrops. I threw a Neumann U87 on top and a Coles 4038 behind them for room ambiance (McQueen, 2022).

To close out Day 1, we moved into the control room where we recorded Joey’s Yamaha keyboard and his bass by patching them directly into the patch bay to capture mono DI signals.

That was the moment Emma asked me to add some layers of guitars and I played some experimental layers just to see what was working.

Day 2: Studio 1 – Valve Vocals and Guitars

Day 2 moved to the larger Studio 1, focusing strictly on polishing the vocals and electric guitars. However, session psychology quickly came into play. Emma arrived highly distracted; her day job was demanding her attention online, and the stress was actively blocking her creative flow. Recognizing this, I called a hard pause. We left the studio, grabbed coffee, and took a "chill pill." Because I had the room booked for the whole day, there was no need to rush a compromised performance.

The Vocal Polish

When we returned, the vibe was reset. Emma wasn't happy with her Day 1 scratch vocals, so we re-recorded everything—leads, harmonies, and backing vocals.

For Emma's lead vocals, I swapped our microphone to the Warm Audio WA-47, a large-diaphragm valve (tube) condenser.

The decision to move away from a standard solid-state microphone was entirely driven by the dark, acoustic nature of the song. As detailed by Hodge (2018) and the LEWITT Content Team (2022), thermionic valve circuits introduce pleasing even-order harmonic distortion and natural, tape-like compression when driven by a vocal. Unlike solid-state microphones, which can sometimes sound overly clinical or precise, the WA-47's tube circuit imparted a warm, saturated, "larger-than-life" vintage character that wrapped perfectly around the acoustic guitars, eliminating the need for heavy digital saturation in the mix phase.

During the takes, Emma expressed some concern about her pitch. I reassured her of a crucial studio reality: if a performance captures the perfect emotion and timing, correcting a note by half a semitone in Melodyne is the right call. The industry needs to stop shaming artists for pitch correction. Preserving an emotionally resonant, top-notch take by applying transparent digital tuning is standard commercial practice, and it yields a far better record than comping together a sterile, perfectly pitched but emotionally dead performance.

Electric Guitars: Trust and Mascara

The session ended with my electric guitar overdubs. Emma demonstrated incredible trust, offering no controlling input and allowing me total creative freedom to track ear candy, lead lines, and ambient chords. I used the Line 6 Pod Go to create my tones and recorded its output without recording DI, I did so as I was sure of the guitar tones and I wanted to commit them then and there. Perks of being the engineer I guess.

The most memorable moment of the day happened when I realized I had left my guitar slide at home. In that moment Emma gave me her Mascara and asked me to try that as a Slide and I did, which actually worked in our favour—a perfect, unconventional end to a highly creative two days.
