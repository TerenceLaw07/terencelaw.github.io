The Human Element: Reflections on Studio Psychology, Roles, and Reality

Looking back and reflecting on all the things I have learnt and realized during my Major Project Reflection

By Terence Law

When you study audio engineering, the curriculum naturally focuses on signal flow, phase coherence, and microphone polar patterns. However, the reality of running a commercial tracking session is vastly different. Through the course of my Major Project, I have spent countless hours in the tracking room with a diverse array of artists. The technical challenges were expected, but the most profound lessons I learned were entirely human.

Here are my key reflections on studio psychology, workflow boundaries, and the reality of making records.

The Importance of Pre-Production and Alignment

One of the most critical takeaways from my recent sessions is that a successful mix is actually secured weeks before the studio date. In the future, especially as I transition into more commercial, paid opportunities, I intend to make live vetting and pre-production non-negotiable. Seeing an artist or an ensemble play live before hitting the studio is invaluable. It ensures that everyone stepping onto the studio floor is fully prepared and capable of executing the arrangement. A studio is a magnifying glass; it highlights both brilliance and under-preparation.

Furthermore, aligning on the ultimate goal of the session is vital. Moving forward, my standard practice will include clear contractual agreements regarding the nature of the project. Approaching a session intended for a commercial release requires a vastly different mental, emotional, and technical investment than simply tracking a rough demo. As engineers, we pour our hearts and finite energy into capturing these sounds, and we want to see those efforts reach the world. Ensuring the project is slated for release guarantees that the mutual effort put in on the studio floor is honored.

Defining the Role: Engineer vs. Producer

The studio is a collaborative space, but ambiguous boundaries can lead to friction, especially in the post-production and mixing phases. I have learned that establishing exact roles and crediting before a session begins is essential for a healthy working relationship.

Are you hiring me purely as a Recording Engineer, or as a Producer? If my role is strictly engineering, my focus is entirely on acoustic capture—placing the right microphones, maintaining pristine gain staging, and serving the artist’s immediate requests. However, if my role bleeds into production, it becomes my responsibility to push the artist, ask for retakes for timing, and critically shape the arrangement. Blurring these lines without prior clarity often leads to mismanaged expectations during the mixing stage. If an engineer is expected to invest the emotional and creative labor of a producer, the crediting and collaborative respect must reflect that from day one.

Technical Epiphanies: Less is More (Until it Isn't)

On the technical side, my philosophy has shifted significantly regarding gear. Sometimes, capturing the perfect tone requires an elaborate, multi-microphone array to isolate specific frequencies. Other times, a single, perfectly placed microphone is all the mix needs.

You don't always need a £3,000 microphone to make a great record. One of the most pleasant technical surprises of my Major Project was the performance of the Rode M5 small-diaphragm condensers. These pencil mics proved to be absolutely top-tier, providing stunning clarity, transient response, and a beautiful stereo image on a variety of acoustic sources. It reinforced the golden rule of audio: trust your ears, not the price tag.

Vibe Over Ego

The most defining factor of a recording session isn't the console, the room, or even the sheer technical skill of the players—it is the vibe. I would choose to work with a humble, passionate musician with a genuine will to create over an artist with a massive ego and flawless technique every single time.

If an artist brings positive energy, warmth, and good intentions into the room, it completely changes the engineering mindset. In those environments, even if I am only the tracking engineer and won't be mixing the final record, I am driven to go above and beyond. I will meticulously craft the stems to make the next mix engineer's life as easy as possible, simply because the artist was a joy to work with. Music is a relationship business; people remember how you made them feel in the room, and good energy always leads to repeat collaboration.

Serving the Vision

Ultimately, unless you are working in a highly sterile, corporate commercial environment, 98% of the time, the artists in front of the glass are pouring their hearts out. They are vulnerable. In those moments, the engineer's personal musical taste does not matter. Our job is to serve their vision, respect their emotional state, and build a sonic environment where they feel safe enough to deliver their best performance.

I don't do this purely for the money; I do it out of a profound love for music and the collaborative process. But I also want the work to mean something. I want to help build records that see the light of day, alongside artists who respect the craft, share the credit, and leave their egos at the door. That is the kind of studio environment I am committed to building.
